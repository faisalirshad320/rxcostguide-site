Biktarvy vs Dovato: cost, coverage & which is right for you

The two most common modern single-tablet HIV regimens — one three-drug, one two-drug — compared on cost and coverage.

Reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS, M.Phil Chemical Pathology PMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-01
The short answer

List prices are both high — Biktarvy about $4,000/month, Dovato about $3,100/month — but almost no one pays list. Both reach $0 for commercially insured patients with their copay cards, both are covered by ADAP for the uninsured, and both fall under the $2,100 Medicare Part D cap. The choice is clinical (three drugs vs two, resistance, other conditions), not about price.

When Biktarvy tends to be the choice

Biktarvy is a three-drug, unboosted single tablet with a very high barrier to resistance and few interactions, which makes it a common first-line pick, including when the full resistance picture is uncertain. Cost-wise it is covered the same way as Dovato.

When Dovato tends to be the choice

Dovato uses two drugs instead of three, which some clinicians prefer to reduce long-term drug exposure, provided resistance testing and hepatitis B status allow it. It lists a little lower, but in practice both are $0 for most patients.

Which is cheaper for you?

Commercial insurance: both reach $0 with their cards — coverage tier decides any small difference. Medicare: both are protected-class Part D drugs under the $2,100 cap. Uninsured: both are free through ADAP or the manufacturer program. Because the out-of-pocket cost is usually identical ($0), choose on clinical grounds with your provider, never on list price.

FAQ

Is Biktarvy or Dovato cheaper?

Dovato lists a little lower (~$3,100 vs ~$4,000/month), but both are $0 for most commercially insured patients and free through ADAP for the uninsured, so real out-of-pocket cost is usually the same.

What is the difference between Biktarvy and Dovato?

Biktarvy is a three-drug single tablet; Dovato is a two-drug single tablet. Both are complete once-daily regimens. Which suits you depends on resistance testing, hepatitis B status and other factors.

Can I switch between them?

Only with your HIV provider. Switching regimens requires checking resistance and other conditions — it should never be done to save money without specialist input, especially since both are usually $0 anyway.
