What mental-health medications cost, and how to pay less

Branded antidepressants and atypical antipsychotics are among the most expensive long-term prescriptions people fill — but they are also where copay cards reach $0 most reliably, and where a single generic (lurasidone) has quietly undercut the whole category.

Reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS, M.Phil Chemical Pathology PMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-05
The short answer

Branded psychiatric drugs run roughly $500 to $1,600 a month at list price. The cheapest route depends almost entirely on insurance: commercially insured patients can usually reach $0–$10 a month with the manufacturer copay card; Medicare patients cannot use those cards and pay their plan cost-share under the $2,100 Part D cap (with Vraylar getting a Medicare-negotiated price effective 2027); uninsured patients should ask about generic lurasidone (the generic of Latuda) and manufacturer patient-assistance programs.

Why the generic matters most here

For years every second-generation antipsychotic was brand-only and expensive. That changed when generic lurasidone (the generic of Latuda) arrived — it is dramatically cheaper than any branded alternative and, for many patients, clinically reasonable. If cost is the deciding factor, generic lurasidone is almost always worth raising with your prescriber before a brand-only drug like Vraylar, Rexulti or Caplyta.

Copay cards vs Medicare

Manufacturer copay cards for psychiatric drugs are unusually generous — several bring commercially insured patients to $0. But by federal rule they cannot be used with Medicare, Medicaid or any government plan. Medicare patients instead rely on their plan's formulary tier, the $2,100 Part D out-of-pocket cap (new for 2025–26), and, for Vraylar specifically, the Medicare-negotiated price that takes effect in 2027.

A note on stopping and switching

Never stop or switch a psychiatric medication to save money without your prescriber. Many of these drugs require tapering, and an abrupt change can cause relapse or withdrawal. Cost is a real constraint worth discussing openly with your doctor — but the clinical decision is always theirs and yours together, not the price tag's.
